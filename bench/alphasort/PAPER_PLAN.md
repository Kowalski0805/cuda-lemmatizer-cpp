**Established results** (all against the real 9.35 M-state trie, checksum-verified):

1. Exact ordering is worthless and, past ~35 M tokens, actively harmful (§3.1,
   §3.4). Ordering precision saturates at **one letter**.
2. Reordering trades stream coalescing for warp uniformity; compaction buys
   both, giving **3.4×** (§3.2). Counters confirm the mechanism directly.
3. **alpha ≥ lenalpha > len** at every scale; length ordering alone is a net
   loss, shown by a within-key-mode controlled experiment (§3.3).
4. Baseline cost per token is **scale-invariant**; ordered variants decay
   toward it. The decay is an L2 effect, not a coalescing effect (§3.4b).
   Separability is confirmed **more strongly than Chapter 3 claims** — ν is
   flat to 2.1 % over 50×, so the whole scale dependence is one scalar per
   algorithm (§3.7). But Eq. (3)'s functional form is **wrong**: it scores
   34.5 % held-out and improves when its inputs are degraded. Replacing the
   hit-rate-weighted latency average with measured latency exposure gives
   **τ ≈ α·ν·σ + β at 13 % with fewer parameters**. Chapter 3 §3.3 must be
   rewritten around (3′).
5. A cheap prefix sort is a **single-pass win up to ~8.2 M tokens**, peaking at
   1.94× near 1.5–2 M (§3.5). Beyond that, ordering needs reuse.
6. Two measurement artifacts found and fixed (§2), each of which had produced a
   self-consistent but false result.
7. The whole effect is **corpus-invariant at fixed token count** (§3.7b).
   Three corpora spanning type/token 0.029–0.163 and hit rate 78–95 % agree
   to within 0.04 ns/token on the baseline and rank every variant
   identically. What looked like a corpus effect was a scale effect.

**The kernel this study optimizes is a fifth of the pipeline.** By Amdahl,
Table 4.1's 3.33× is worth **1.18× end to end**, the single-pass 1.94× is

**So the fusion was implemented — `src/cuda/fused_lemmatize.cu` — and it
removes all four surrounding stages, not just the split.** A token is a span
of the input sentence and a lemma is a span of the trie's lemma buffer, so
nothing between them needs to be a cuDF column. Five kernels: count fields,
scan, emit (pointer, length) per token, **the production traversal unchanged**,
scan lemma lengths, copy. Only one touches the trie.

| tokens/call | staged ms | fused ms | speedup | identical |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 200 K | 12.49 | 1.70 | **7.35×** | yes |
| 1 M | 22.81 | 4.75 | **4.80×** | yes |
| 4 M | 46.04 | 10.69 | **4.31×** | yes |
| 10 M | 99.30 | 23.37 | **4.25×** | yes |

Output is compared bit for bit on device at every measurement, and splitting
semantics are verified separately against `split_record`/`join_list_elements`
on 14 pathological inputs (empty strings; leading, trailing and consecutive
separators; out-of-dictionary tokens) — `./batch_probe --selftest`.

At 4 M tokens the fused path completes the whole operation in 10.69 ms, about
what the traversal *alone* cost before (10.25 ms). The Amdahl ceiling from
Table 5.1 is 4.53× at that size, so the measured 4.31× realizes **95 %** of it.

**The two are complementary, and the order matters.** Fusion removes the
plumbing and leaves the traversal as almost the whole remaining cost — so
Chapter 4's ordering results, capped at 1.18× against the staged pipeline,
apply to nearly the full runtime of the fused one. Fusion first, ordering
second; ordering is worth much more after fusion than before it.

## 2. A measurement artifact that had to be fixed first

The first run at scale produced a clean monotone "improvement" across variants.
It was entirely fake. Without root, `nvidia-smi -lgc` is refused, and an idle
GPU drops to **210 MHz core / 405 MHz memory** against 3105 / 11501 max.
Variant 1's ~6 s host `std::sort` parks the clocks; every variant timed after
it measures a ramping GPU. The apparent ranking tracked *execution order*.

Fixed by splitting the harness: build every permutation first (all host work
happens there), then warm up under load and time the kernels **round-robin over
R rounds**, reporting per-variant medians plus max/min as a drift indicator.
Residual spread is ~1.09, and `best` (min) reproduces the same ranking, so the
conclusions are robust to it.

A second, subtler instance of the same discipline problem: `prep` was initially
timed **once** while `lookup` was a median over rounds. The first build of each
ordering absorbs CUDA module load and first-`cudaMalloc` cost, which is
per-process rather than per-batch, so single-shot prep inflated the ordering
cost and produced a non-monotone one-shot curve (variant 3 read 1.09× at 6 M
but 0.73× at 8 M). With median-of-5 prep (`--prepreps`) the curves are smooth
and single-peaked, and the estimated break-even moved from ~6.4 M to ~8.2 M
tokens — a 28 % shift in the paper's headline recommendation.

**Both belong in the paper's methodology section** — they are exactly the traps
a reviewer would ask about, and most GPU-sorting papers address neither.

**Clocks can, after all, be pinned** — but not from WSL, whose `nvidia-smi` is
a shim that answers queries and refuses management operations ("Unknown
Error"). From an elevated Windows PowerShell, `nvidia-smi -lgc 2400,2400` and
`-lmc 11501` both succeed and are visible from WSL immediately. Verified at
2400 MHz core / 11501 MHz memory (`results/locked_fiction_alpha.txt`).

Two findings from the pinned run, both worth reporting:

1. **The ranking is invariant** and the headline improves slightly
   (6 sort+compact 3.38× → 3.66×). No conclusion depends on clock state, which
   retrospectively validates the round-robin design.
2. **Reported timings are reproducible to under 1 %.** Two independent runs at
   the same operating point (2730 and 2745 MHz) agree within 0.7 % on both
   medians and best-of times for every variant (baseline 10.101 / 10.069 ms
   median; variant 6 3.016 / 3.023 ms). Either statistic is safe to quote.

   **The max/min column, however, is not reproducible and must not be
   interpreted as a variance measure.** It is an extremum over nine rounds, so
   one outlier round moves it: variant 6 recorded 1.010, 1.010, 1.062 and 1.260
   across four locked runs, variant 4 recorded 1.175, 1.172, 1.169 and 1.075.
   An earlier draft of this section claimed that pinning tightened the spread
   only for the compacted variants and inferred that their variance was
   intrinsic cache-state inheritance; that inference rested on two runs of a
   noisy statistic and is withdrawn. Keep max/min as a coarse drift alarm, not
   as an error bar — for a real error bar, report the interquartile range over
   more rounds.

A caveat on the operating point: pinning memory at maximum while pinning core
*below* its opportunistic boost tilts the balance toward memory-bound variants,
so the locked point mildly affects the ratios and not merely the noise
(absolute times rose ~13 %, speedups rose a few points).

### 2.1 The clock-sensitivity experiment (an unplanned result)

Repeating the run at a second core clock to demonstrate invariance produced
something more useful. Requested 2805 MHz; the card power-capped at 320 W and
settled at **2730 MHz**, a 13.75 % increase over 2400. Memory pinned at 11501
throughout (`results/locked2805_fiction_alpha.txt`).

| variant | t @2400 | t @2730 | speedup @2400 | speedup @2730 | sensitivity |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 baseline | 11.412 | 10.101 | 1.00× | 1.00× | **0.94** |
| 2 gpu-sort-8B | 10.070 | 9.937 | 1.13× | 1.02× | 0.10 |
| 2b gpu-full | 11.077 | 10.468 | 1.03× | 0.96× | 0.42 |
| 3 gpu-prefix | 8.205 | 8.052 | 1.39× | 1.25× | 0.14 |
| 4 gpu-partition | 6.265 | 5.933 | 1.82× | 1.70× | 0.41 |
| 6 sort+compact | 3.122 | 3.016 | 3.66× | 3.35× | 0.26 |
| 6b coarse+compact | 3.382 | 3.146 | 3.37× | 3.21× | 0.55 † |

Sensitivity = (t₂₄₀₀/t₂₇₃₀ − 1) / (f₂₇₃₀/f₂₄₀₀ − 1); 1.0 is perfect scaling
with core clock, 0 is complete insensitivity.

**The ranking is identical at both operating points**, which closes the
robustness objection. More valuably, the sensitivities discriminate the model's
factors directly: the baseline scales with core clock at **0.94 — essentially
perfectly — so it is issue/latency-bound**, exactly as its 72.8 % branch
uniformity predicts, while **every ordered variant scales at ≤ 0.42, so all are
memory-bound**. This is an independent confirmation of the §3.4b claim that the
baseline is limited by the divergence factor `D` and the ordered variants by
the memory factor `C`, obtained without any profiler counters. It also explains
why the apparent speedups rise at the lower clock: reducing core frequency
penalises only the baseline.

**Three-point curve.** Extended to 1800 MHz (`results/locked1800_*.txt`),
spanning a 1.52× clock range. Best-of times, since the 1800 median was
noise-inflated (max/min 1.150):

| variant | branch unif % | t@1800 | t@2400 | t@2730 | sensitivity |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 baseline | 72.8 | 15.20 | 11.41 | 10.10 | **0.98** |
| 4 gpu-partition | 78.5 | 7.27 | 6.26 | 5.91 | 0.45 |
| 6b coarse+compact | 80.0 | 4.00 | 3.37 | 3.14 | 0.52 |
| 3 gpu-prefix | 83.9 | 8.62 | 8.18 | 8.00 | 0.15 |
| 6 sort+compact | 91.5 | 3.36 | 3.10 | 3.01 | 0.23 |
| 2 gpu-sort-8B | 91.5 | 10.37 | 10.05 | 9.92 | 0.09 |
| 2b gpu-full | 98.5 | 10.85 | 10.25 | 10.11 | 0.14 |

Rows are sorted by branch uniformity, which makes the structure visible:
**core-clock sensitivity falls as warp uniformity rises.** The three
least-uniform variants (72.8 / 78.5 / 80.0 %) carry the three highest
sensitivities (0.98 / 0.45 / 0.53); the three most uniform (91.5 / 91.5 /
98.5 %) the lowest (0.09 / 0.23 / 0.14). Divergence implies serialized
instruction issue, which is exactly what scales with core frequency, so this is
a direct empirical link between the model's `D` factor and clock scaling —
established without profiler counters. The relation is a trend, not a fit:
variants 2 and 6 share 91.5 % uniformity yet differ (0.09 vs 0.23), so
divergence is a strong but not sole determinant. 6b's sensitivity was
recomputed from a clean repeat run at 2745 MHz (0.52 against 0.53 from the
originally flagged run), so the figure stands.

**Consequence for reporting.** The headline speedup is operating-point
dependent — variant 6 yields 4.86× at 1800 MHz, 3.66× at 2400 and 3.35× at
2730 — because reducing core frequency penalises only the (issue-bound)
baseline. Every quoted speedup must therefore carry its clock. Recommended
operating point for all final numbers: **2730 MHz core / 11501 MHz memory**,
which reproduces unpinned behaviour (baseline 10.10 vs 10.07 ms unpinned) and
so keeps the existing 21-run sweep consistent without re-running it.

## 3. Findings

### 3.1 The central result: two opposing effects

Ordering precision has *opposite* signs on the two memory streams. Profiler
counters, fiction 18.3 M tokens, `--key alpha`:

| variant | DRAM read | L1 hit % | L2 hit % | branch unif % | lookup ms | speedup |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 baseline | **0.40 GB** | **69.0** | **95.4** | 72.8 | 10.07 | 1.00× |
| 4 partition (1 letter) | 1.35 GB | 62.0 | 84.8 | 78.6 | 5.86 | 1.73× |
| 3 prefix (2 letters) | 1.45 GB | 56.2 | 80.7 | 82.6 | 8.02 | 1.27× |
| 2 gpu-sort (4 letters) | 1.48 GB | 38.3 | 77.8 | 91.8 | 9.91 | 1.03× |
| 2b gpu-full (exact) | 1.48 GB | 25.5 | 77.3 | **98.5** | 10.07 | 1.01× |

As precision rises, warp uniformity rises monotonically (72.8 → 98.5 %) and
cache hit rate falls monotonically (69.0 → 25.5 %). Sorting buys path sharing
and pays **3.7× DRAM traffic** for it.

The excess is arithmetically attributable to the *input strings*, not the trie:
18.3 M words × ~2 sectors × 32 B ≈ 1.17 GB gathered, versus ~200 MB when the
corpus streams in natural order — against a measured excess of ~1.08 GB.
Reading words through a permutation destroys coalescing that natural order gets
for free.

### 3.2 The fix, and the paper's engineering payload

Materialise the words in permuted order once (variant 6: sort → gather →
compact, all transient device buffers; the corpus on disk and the output order
are untouched):

| variant | prep ms | lookup ms | speedup | break-even reuses |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 baseline | 0.0 | 10.07 | 1.00× | — |
| 4 partition | 7.4 | 5.86 | 1.73× | 1.8 |
| **6 sort+compact** | 20.0 | **3.02** | **3.38×** | 2.8 |
| **6b sort-2B+compact** | 11.4 | 3.15 | 3.24× | 1.6 |

6.1 Gwords/s on the real trie. And the saturation result survives in the regime
where it matters: a **2-byte** key (one letter) captures 3.24× of the 3.38×
available from the full 8-byte key, at 57 % of the prep cost.

Profiler counters for the same seven orderings (`results/ncu_compact.csv`,
fiction 18.3 M) show the mechanism directly rather than by inference:

| variant | sect/req | branch unif % | L1 hit % | L2 hit % | DRAM read | lookup ms |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 baseline | 5.63 | 72.78 | 69.0 | 95.5 | 0.40 GB | 10.07 |
| 2 gpu-sort-8B | 4.91 | 91.78 | 38.3 | 77.5 | 1.48 GB | 9.91 |
| 2b gpu-full | 5.40 | 98.45 | 25.3 | 77.6 | 1.47 GB | 10.07 |
| 3 gpu-prefix | 5.09 | 82.57 | 56.2 | 80.7 | 1.47 GB | 8.02 |
| 4 gpu-partition | 5.42 | 78.61 | 62.0 | 84.8 | 1.35 GB | 5.86 |
| **6 sort+compact** | **2.93** | **91.72** | **81.9** | 68.5 | **0.38 GB** | **3.02** |
| 6b coarse+compact | 4.53 | 78.60 | 77.8 | 91.2 | 0.38 GB | 3.20 |

Variant 6 carries **sorting's branch uniformity (91.72 vs 2's 91.78) at *less*
DRAM traffic than the unsorted baseline (0.38 vs 0.40 GB)**, with the lowest
sectors-per-request in the study (2.93 vs 5.63). Compaction restores coalescing
without giving back the uniformity sorting bought — the two effects are
genuinely separable and 6 obtains both. This table is the paper's central
figure; everything else is a curve through it.

Two details to explain rather than hide:

- Variant 6's **L2 hit rate is the lowest of all (68.5 %)** while its absolute
  DRAM traffic is also the lowest. Hit rate is a ratio: with sectors/request
  down to 2.93 there are far fewer requests in total, so the residual misses
  are a larger fraction of a much smaller number. Report absolute traffic
  alongside any hit rate, or the table reads backwards.
- 6b has clearly worse coalescing than 6 (4.53 vs 2.93 sect/req) and lower
  uniformity (78.60 vs 91.72), yet performs within 6 % of it — and beats it
  beyond 20 M tokens (§3.4). Its compensation is L2: 91.2 % vs 68.5 %. Coarse
  ordering keeps the trie working set concentrated where fine ordering sweeps
  the whole vocabulary. The two strategies reach the same throughput by
  different routes, which is why the optimum shifts with scale.

### 3.3 alpha vs len — the paper's title axis

Full sweeps for all three key modes, 1 M → 50 M
(`results/scale_{alpha,len,lenalpha}.csv`). Lookup speedup vs baseline:

| tokens | alpha best | len best | lenalpha best |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 M | **6.15×** (6) | 2.40× (2b) | 5.88× (6) |
| 2 M | **5.14×** (6) | 2.50× (2b) | 5.96× (6) |
| 5 M | **5.89×** (6) | 1.42× (2b) | 6.52× (6) |
| 20 M | **3.36×** (6) | 0.95× (2b) | 2.98× (6) |
| 50 M | **3.15×** (6b) | 0.93× (6b) | 2.26× (6) |

alpha ≥ lenalpha > len at every scale, and **under `len` nothing beats the
baseline at all beyond 10 M tokens**.

**The controlled experiment.** Under `--key len`, variant 2b is the sole winner
(2.50× at 2 M) while every other ordering loses (0.65–1.16×). That is not an
anomaly: `refineOffset(KEY_LEN, d) = 8(d−1)`, so 2b's refinement passes sort by
the actual bytes *within* each length class and it silently becomes exact
(len, alpha) ordering. Same length-major key, same code path, one difference —
whether alphabetic refinement happens — and the result flips from 0.88× to
2.50×. This is the mechanism claim as a controlled experiment rather than a
comparison across key modes, and it is the strongest single piece of evidence
in the study.

**The stream decomposition, obtained free.** Under `len` the ordering provides
no path sharing whatsoever, so variant 6b's 1.16× at 2 M is *pure coalescing
gain* from compaction. The same variant under `alpha` gives 3.64×. The two
effects separate cleanly and compose multiplicatively:

    coalescing ≈ 1.16×      path sharing ≈ 3.64 / 1.16 ≈ 3.14×

This is what Phase A item 6 (a separate isolation experiment) was going to
measure; the len sweep already provides it, with the advantage that both
numbers come from the same kernel on the same data.

**Why length-major keys fail — the bit-budget framing.** Because only the top
~2 bytes of the key measurably affect performance (§3.2 saturation), those bits
decide everything. Spending them on length rather than on the first letter is
what destroys the cheap strategies: under `lenalpha`, variant 6 still reaches
5.88× (it keeps 7 alpha bytes below the length byte), but 6b collapses to 1.55×
and partition to 0.85×, because 2 bytes of a length-major key is essentially
just the length.

So the alpha/len question is not "which grouping helps?" but **"what do you
spend the top bits of the key on?"** — path sharing or trip-count uniformity.
The measurement says path sharing, decisively, and says trip-count uniformity
is worth less than nothing because it displaces the former. That single
sentence is the paper's thesis.

### 3.4 The scale law (sweep: wiki, 1 M → 50 M tokens, one corpus)

Per-token lookup cost, ns/token — the table the paper is built on.
**Medians of five launches as of 2026-09-07**; the single-run values this table
used to carry are in brackets where they differed:

| tokens | uniq | 0 base | 2 sort-8B | 3 prefix | 4 partition | 6 s+c | 6b coarse+c |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 M | 143 K | 0.56 | 0.18 | 0.21 | 0.27 [0.28] | 0.09 | 0.15 |
| 5 M | 425 K | 0.54 | 0.31 | 0.28 | 0.30 [0.29] | 0.09 | 0.14 |
| 20 M | 1.14 M | 0.53 [0.54] | 0.50 | 0.42 | 0.35 [0.36] | 0.16 | 0.16 |
| 50 M | 2.17 M | 0.52 | 0.55 [0.57] | 0.45 [0.46] | 0.36 [0.38] | 0.21 | **0.17** [0.16] |

Intermediate scales, medians of five (used by Fig 4.2):
2 M — 0.547 / 0.170 / 0.193 / 0.260 / 0.106 / 0.149;
10 M — 0.538 / 0.429 / 0.364 / 0.333 / 0.116 / 0.148;
35 M — 0.524 / 0.530 / 0.441 / 0.357 / 0.193 / 0.164.

Three facts, each worth a figure:

1. **The baseline is scale-invariant.** 0.52–0.56 ns/token across a 50× range.
   Natural-order lookup is not cache-bound at all — it is divergence/latency
   bound, already at its asymptote by 1 M tokens. This is why it is so hard to
   beat, and it reframes the whole problem.
2. **Every ordering degrades with scale, and fine ordering degrades fastest.**
   Variant 2 goes 0.18 → 0.55 and *crosses the baseline* near **32 M tokens**
   (was interpolated at 27 M before the medians re-measurement): at 35 M and
   50 M, exact 8-byte sorting is a **net loss even ignoring prep** (0.99×,
   0.95×). Ordering precision is not merely useless at scale — it is harmful.
3. **Coarse ordering + compaction is the only scale-invariant strategy.**
   Variant 6b holds 0.15 → 0.17 ns/token over the full range, and **overtakes
   fine ordering (6) at ~20 M tokens**, ending at 3.15× where 6 has decayed to
   2.47×. The optimum precision is one letter, at every scale tested.

### 3.4b Why it decays — and the model that falls out

Counters at 1 M / 20 M / 50 M on the same corpus
(`results/ncu_scale_*.csv`, `parse_ncu.py`) separate the effects cleanly:

| counter | 1 M | 20 M | 50 M | verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| sect/req, baseline | 5.82 | 5.70 | 5.64 | flat |
| sect/req, 2 sort | 4.72 | 4.79 | 4.80 | flat |
| sect/req, 6 s+c | 3.10 | 3.09 | 3.08 | flat |
| branch unif, 2 sort | 90.95 | 91.54 | 91.68 | flat |
| branch unif, 6b | 79.61 | 79.95 | 80.01 | flat |
| **L2 hit, 2 sort** | 89.59 | 78.94 | 79.35 | **−10 pp** |
| **L2 hit, 3 prefix** | 91.98 | 81.34 | 80.73 | **−11 pp** |
| **L2 hit, 6 s+c** | 55.30 | 65.57 | 66.00 | **+11 pp** |
| **L2 hit, 6b** | 83.12 | 88.77 | 89.30 | **+6 pp** |
| L2 hit, baseline | 94.11 | 95.16 | 95.17 | flat |

**Coalescing and branch uniformity are scale-invariant across a 50× range** —
both are pure functions of ordering precision. L2 hit rate is the *only*
counter that moves with scale, and it moves in opposite directions for ordered
(−10 pp) versus compacted (+6…11 pp) variants. So §3.4's decay is an **L2
capacity effect, not a coalescing effect**.

Per-token DRAM traffic (20 M / 50 M, where GB rounding is trustworthy; the 1 M
point is dominated by the fixed 260 MB trie footprint):

| variant | B/token @20 M | B/token @50 M |
|---|---|---|
| 0 baseline | 23.5 | 23.6 |
| 2 gpu-sort-8B | 79.0 | 80.8 |
| 4 gpu-partition | 75.0 | 74.8 |
| 6 sort+compact | 23.0 | **21.8** |

**The three-factor model.** These counters factor the cost into terms with
disjoint dependencies, which is exactly what Chapter 3's Eqs. (3)–(7) lack:

    time/token  ≈  R(precision) × C(working set) × D(precision)

- `R` — requests per token, from sectors/request. Function of **ordering
  precision only**; measured flat in scale.
- `C` — cost per request, driven by L2 hit rate. Function of **working-set size
  only**; measured flat in precision-independent fashion for the baseline and
  monotone in scale for the rest.
- `D` — divergence multiplier, from branch uniformity. Function of **ordering
  precision only**; measured flat in scale.

Two of three factors depend only on precision and the third only on scale.
That separability is why the optimum precision shifts with corpus size at all,
and it makes the model *predictive*: measure `R` and `D` once at any convenient
size, measure `C` from the working set, and the optimum follows. Fitting and
validating this against the 21-run sweep is Phase C's main task.

### 3.5 When does it pay on a single pass?

`baseline_lookup / (prep + lookup)`; > 1.00 means ordering wins with **no**
reuse at all. Fine grid, 10 points, median-of-5 prep (`results/scale_fine2.csv`):

| tokens | 2 sort-8B | 3 prefix | 4 partition | 6 s+c | 6b coarse+c |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.5 M | 1.15× | 1.55× | 0.82× | 0.34× | 0.37× |
| 1.0 M | 1.57× | 1.87× | 0.89× | 0.50× | 0.55× |
| **1.5 M** | **1.69×** | **1.94×** | 0.88× | 0.55× | 0.59× |
| 2 M | 1.65× | 1.93× | 0.89× | 0.57× | 0.63× |
| 3 M | 1.41× | 1.75× | 0.88× | 0.56× | 0.65× |
| 4 M | 1.03× | 1.38× | 0.84× | 0.52× | 0.66× |
| 5 M | 0.85× | 1.20× | 0.81× | 0.51× | 0.66× |
| 6 M | 0.78× | 1.10× | 0.79× | 0.48× | 0.64× |
| 8 M | 0.71× | 1.01× | 0.78× | 0.48× | 0.64× |
| 10 M | 0.68× | 0.95× | 0.77× | 0.47× | 0.66× |

Both sorting variants are **single-peaked with an interior optimum at ~1.5 M
tokens**. Below it, fixed prep overheads outweigh a small absolute gain; above
it, the ordered lookup decays per §3.4. Interpolated one-shot break-even:

- **3 gpu-prefix: wins up to ~8.2 M tokens, peak 1.94×**
- 2 gpu-sort-8B: wins up to ~4.1 M tokens, peak 1.69×
- 2b, 4, 6, 6b: never win one-shot at any size tested

So there is a **batch-size operating window** — roughly 1–8 M tokens, best at
1.5–2 M — in which a cheap prefix sort is free money, and outside which
ordering must be amortised over reuse. That window is the paper's single most
actionable output, and it is a much better result than the earlier "never wins
one-shot", which came from measuring at one size (18 M) and from single-shot
prep timing.

The two regimes:

- **batch 1–8 M tokens, single pass** → variant 3, cheap prefix sort, no
  compaction. Up to 1.94×, no reuse needed.
- **resident corpus, ≥ 2 passes, any scale** → variant 6b, coarse sort +
  compaction. 3.15–3.9×, scale-invariant.

Nothing else is ever the right answer, which is a pleasingly sharp conclusion
for a paper.

Still true, and still worth stating: a host-fed pipeline is transfer-bound
(~200 MB at ~12 GB/s ≈ 17 ms vs 10 ms lookup at 18 M tokens), so end-to-end
gains there are bounded by PCIe regardless of ordering.

### 3.7 Validating the cost model — Eq. (3) is the wrong form, and the right one is simpler

`run_ncu_ext.sh` profiles all seven variants at all seven swept scales with ten
counters — **49 observations**, validated by leave-one-scale-out: every fit
sees six corpus sizes and predicts the seventh. Two counters removed the
substitutions the first attempt could not avoid:

| was | is now |
|---|---|
| ν inferred from the DRAM identity | `l1tex__t_sectors_...op_ld.sum / n`, counted |
| e as branch-target uniformity (a proxy) | `smsp__thread_inst_executed_per_inst_executed.ratio / 32`, the quantity itself |

The proxy was badly wrong: true warp efficiency for the baseline is **0.312**,
not the 0.728 that branch uniformity reported. Ordering raises it to 0.946 —
a threefold improvement, a far stronger statement of the path-sharing
mechanism than the uniformity figures in §3.1 and Table 4.2 make. **Those
tables should be restated on the true counter.**

A second new measurement is more important still. `l1tex__t_requests` counts
requests *after* intra-warp coalescing, so path sharing is directly visible:
requests per token fall **9.59 → 2.88** as ordering precision rises. That is
path sharing measured, not inferred.

**Eq. (3) fails, and fails structurally.** Held-out MAPE 34.5 %, and only 5 of
7 folds pick the winning algorithm. The decisive evidence that the *form* is
wrong rather than the inputs noisy: accuracy **improves when the factors are
degraded** — 33.1 % with correctly measured ν and e, 23.5 % with both old
proxies, 28.8 % with the divergence factor deleted. A model that gets better
when you feed it worse measurements is not measuring what it claims.

**The missing quantity is latency exposure**, `smsp__average_warps_issue
_stalled_long_scoreboard_per_issue_active.ratio` — call it σ. Eq. (3) reaches
for it indirectly through a hit-rate-weighted average latency, which cannot
work, because an average latency says nothing about how much of it is hidden.

| model | params | held-out MAPE | argmin |
|---|---|---|---|
| Eq. (3), three-level `L_eff` | 3 | 34.5 % | 5/7 |
| α·ν·σ | 1 | 20.5 % | 5/7 |
| **α·ν·σ + β** | **2** | **13.1 %** | 5/7 |
| α·ν·σ/e + β (divergence kept) | 2 | 23.9 % | 5/7 |
| α·ν·σ + β + DRAM term | 3 | 11.6 % | 5/7 |
| on requests rather than sectors | 3 | 9.6 % | 6/7 |

So the replacement is

    τ(π, n) ≈ α · ν(π) · σ(π, n) + β                                (3′)

**with fewer parameters and a third of the error.** It subsumes the divergence
factor — dividing by e makes the fit *worse* (23.9 % against 13.1 %), so warp
efficiency is not an independent term but a contributor to how much latency a
warp exposes. The cache hit rates likewise stop earning their place once σ is
present.

**Proposition 2 survives in a stronger form than it was stated.** ν is flat to
**2.1 %** across a 50× range for every algorithm, so the entire scale
dependence of the problem lives in one scalar per algorithm — and that
scalar's own scale sensitivity reproduces the paper's ranking with no
reference to timing whatsoever:

| variant | σ, 1 M → 50 M | CV | §3.4 verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 baseline | 80.0 → 75.5 | **1.7 %** | scale-invariant ✓ |
| 6b coarse+compact | 23.4 → 21.8 | **7.8 %** | scale-invariant ✓ |
| 4 partition | 45.2 → 53.7 | 9.1 % | mild decay ✓ |
| 3 prefix | 41.7 → 85.4 | 27.5 % | decays ✓ |
| 6 sort+compact | 28.1 → 54.2 | 35.0 % | decays ✓ |
| 2 sort-8B | 50.1 → 155.0 | 38.7 % | decays, crosses baseline ✓ |
| 2b exact | 60.1 → 223.7 | **43.3 %** | worst ✓ |

**Two things must be conceded in the chapter.** σ is obtained from the kernel
it describes, so (3′) explains and interpolates but does not predict before the
fact; a predictive form needs σ modelled from precision and working-set size,
and this study measures the endpoints of that chain (scale → L2 residency →
latency exposure → time) but not its middle link. And the A6/A6b crossover
stays at the edge of resolution — the two differ by under 20 % beyond 35 M
tokens and no model family calls it stably — so it must not be claimed as a
prediction.

**Methodological finding worth its own paragraph.** The first attempt read ν as
*sectors per request*, the counter closest to hand. Different quantity: it
omits how many requests a token issues, which differs fourfold between
compacted and permuted variants. Under that substitution the fit returns
**negative DRAM latency** and does worse than predicting the mean.
`validate_model_21pt.py` PART 5 reproduces the failure deliberately.

`run_corpus_sweep.sh` holds token count fixed and varies the corpus, so the

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 750 K | articles | 0.0291 | 77.9 | 0.585 | 3.12× | 2.84× | 2.22× | **7.51×** | 3.92× |

| 750 K | wiki | 0.1541 | 83.7 | 0.571 | 3.07× | 2.68× | 2.07× | 6.28× | 3.80× |

independent data.

### 3.6 Open anomalies

- ~~`5 streaming+bins` (24.3 ms) is *slower* than `5 streaming-nobins`
  (21.9 ms)~~ **CONFIRMED AND WORSE, 2026-09-07.** Re-measured over 14
  independent runs across seven scales and two toolkits: the penalty is
  sign-consistent 14/14, mean **+20.6 %**, range +7.4 % to +64.7 %. It is not a
  fixed ~11 % oddity but grows with scale — +8 to +17 % to 35 M, then **+52.8 %
  and +64.7 % at 50 M**. Partition work sits on the copy stream, serialised
  with H2D. Either move it to a third stream or accept and explain it; the
  write-up should state the scale dependence, which the current +11 % framing
  understates badly.
- Variant 2b needs **22 refinement passes** on fiction vs 6 on articles. The
  loop runs until the longest colliding group resolves, and the loader admits
  tokens up to 250 B, so a handful of pathological tokens set the cost. Report
  the distribution, or cap word length and say so.
- ~~`1 cpu-sort` lands at 0.89–0.93×~~ **CONFIRMED, 2026-09-07.** Three fresh
  runs at 18.3 M fiction give 0.92, 0.88, 0.92× — the recorded range exactly.
  A1 and A2b produce the *same* exact ordering, yet A1 is 8–12 % slower, and
  A1's own run-to-run spread is **5.2 %** where every other variant sits at
  ≤0.34 %. That variance asymmetry supports the clock-parking explanation
  already in §3.6: the multi-second host sort parks the clocks and A1's lookup
  is timed on a ramping device. Note the 0.89 is a *ratio*, not a percentage;
  the old summary line above mis-stated it as "0.89 %". At 1 M and 5 M, where
  ordering still pays, A1 tracks A2b to 0.4 % and 5.7 % and is 2.94× and 1.59×
  *faster* than baseline — the sub-unity ratio appears only at 18.3 M, where
  exact ordering is a net loss anyway, which is the paper's main finding rather
  than an anomaly.

## 4. Does it suit the codebase?

Yes, and variant 6 maps onto existing cuDF operations rather than new kernels.
Production path today (`GpuLemmatizer.cpp` → `lemmatize_batch`):

```
split_record → explode_position → lookup_kernel(column_device_view) → groupby/collect → join
```

Variant 6 slots in between `explode_position` and the lookup:

1. key kernel over the words `column_device_view` → `uint64` keys
2. `cub::DeviceRadixSort::SortPairs` → `perm`
3. **`cudf::gather(words, perm)`** — cuDF already does the string compaction
   efficiently; this is step 6's gather, for free
4. `lookup_kernel` on the gathered column, writing `out[perm[i]]`
5. no un-gather: results already scatter to original positions

~50 lines in `lemmatizer.cpp`, no new kernels beyond the key builder. Costs:
`cudf::gather` allocates a full copy of the chars (≈2× input column memory).

**Which variant to integrate depends on the JNI batch size**, per §3.5. If
batches are ≲5 M words — likely, since they arrive as sentence columns from
Java — then steps 1–2 alone (prefix sort, *skipping* the gather in step 3) are
a net win on a single pass, up to 1.85× at 2 M words, with no reuse and no
extra memory beyond the perm array. That is the cheap integration and it should
be measured first. The full sort+gather path only pays for resident corpora
processed more than twice, so it belongs behind a flag.

Action: instrument the existing JNI path to log actual batch sizes before
deciding.
